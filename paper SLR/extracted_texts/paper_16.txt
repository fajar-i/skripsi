Modeling Student Performance Using Feature
Crosses Information for Knowledge Tracing
Lixiang Xu , Member , IEEE, Zhanlong Wang , Suojuan Zhang ,X i nY u a n , Member , IEEE,
Minjuan Wang , Member , IEEE, and Enhong Chen , Fellow, IEEE
Abstract—Knowledge tracing (KT) is an intelligent educational
technology used to model students’ learning progress and mas-
tery in adaptive learning environments for personalized educa-
tion. Despite utilizing deep learning models in KT, current ap-
proaches often oversimplify students’ exercise records into knowl-
edge sequences, which fail to explore the rich information within
individual questions. In addition, existing KT models tend to
neglect the complex, higher order relationships between ques-
tions and latent concepts. Therefore, we introduce a novel model
called feature crosses information-based KT (FCIKT) to explore
the intricate interplay between questions, latent concepts, and
question difﬁculties. FCIKT utilizes a fusion module to perform
feature crosses operations on questions, integrating information
from our constructed multirelational heterogeneous graph using
graph convolutional networks. We deployed a multihead attention
mechanism, which enriches the static embedding representations
of questions and concepts with dynamic semantic information to
simulate real-world scenarios of problem-solving. We also used
gated recurrent units to dynamically capture and update the stu-
dents’ knowledge state for ﬁnal prediction. Extensive experiments
demonstrated the validity and interpretability of our proposed
model.
Index Terms —Graph neural network (GNN), intelligent
education, knowledge tracing (KT), recurrent neural network
(RNN).

I. I NTRODUCTION
E
DUCA TORS employ teaching to effectively introduce and
impart knowledge of novel concepts, theories, and prac-
tices to students, facilitating comprehensive understanding and
mastery. The central aspect of teaching activities is the ability
of educators to monitor and assess students’ real-time progress.
This monitoring is crucial allowing for timely adjustments to
teaching strategies, pacing, and materials, thereby optimizing
the growth of knowledge and the enhancement of competence
in students [1]. Traditionally, educators primarily relied on di-
rect classroom interaction and conventional teaching methods.
However, advancements in artiﬁcial intelligence technology, the
proliferation of massive open online courses [2], and the rapid
development of smart education have signiﬁcantly transformed
traditional educational approaches[3]. For instance, the COVID-
19 pandemic shifted in this landscape, in which conventional
ofﬂine education rapidly transitioned to online platforms. This
shift forced educational institutions, businesses, and individuals
to embrace online education and expedite the digital transfor-
mation of the education sector [4]. Consequently, the utilization
of educational data mining has become increasingly prominent
in enhancing education systems. These techniques monitor stu-
dents’ knowledge mastery by analyzing data from their previous
exercise records, enabling more effective assessment of learning
outcomes and personalized learning plans [5], [6]. This process
is commonly referred to as knowledge tracing (KT) [7].
KT was ﬁrst introduced in the domain of cognitive mod-
eling and intelligent tutoring by Corbett and Anderson [8].
Subsequently, this ﬁeld has evolved, producing a diverse range

duced collaborative KT (CoKT), a novel model that incorporates
both intrastudent and interstudent information for KT tasks.
The interstudent information is gathered by retrieving students
who share similar question answering experiences using a se-
quence retrieval mechanism. Subsequently, various variants of
the KT model have emerged [14], considering diverse proper-
ties inherent in the learning sequences, leading to promising
outcomes.
While the RNN-based model demonstrates proﬁciency in
handling sequential data, it predominantly emphasizes the stu-
dents’ latest exercise records, disregarding their performance on
earlier questions. In accordance with pedagogical conventions,
it is recognized that students may initially provide an incorrect
response to a question. However, with subsequent repetitions of
the same question, they acquire the ability to respond correctly,
indicating an improved level of knowledge mastery compared
with their initial correct response. As it is plausible to expect
that students will retain their prior experience when attempting
the question a second time. Graph-based KT (GKT) emerged.
Nakagawa et al. [15] proposed GKT to potentially structure a
question as a graph, where the requirements for mastering the
question are divided into KCs, called nodes of the graph, and
these KCs have certain dependencies, called edges. It is widely
recognized that integrating a priori knowledge concerning the
graph-based structure of data [16] into a model enhances both
its performance and interpretability. Therefore, the integration of
graph-structured data that amalgamate questions and concepts
has the potential to enhance the effectiveness of KT models.
Despite the success of previous work, there are still some
important limitations. First, GKT does not speciﬁcally analyze
what types of graphs are more suitable as inputs to the model.
The similarity calculation employed by the retrieval mechanism
in CoKT is relatively simplistic, focusing solely on the students’
historical exercise sequences. This approach may overlook es-
sential contextual information, such as question difﬁculty and in-
teractions between different concepts. In addition, certain GKT
methods in existence lack comprehensive consideration of the
relationships between questions and KCs. When students engage
in real-world problem solving, it becomes evident that questions
and KCs exhibit a stronger “and” relationship rather than an “or”
relationship. Higher order feature crosses can precisely realize
the extraction of “and” relationship information between con-
cepts. As illustrated in Fig.1, we analyzed the exercise sequences
of three students. Bill successfully completed three questions
related to multiplication and division but encountered an error on
a trigonometry question. It is reasonable to conclude that the like-
lihood of Bill correctly answering Q5 is low, given the absence
of assurance regarding his mastery of trigonometry. In contrast,
Tom demonstrated the ability to answer questions pertaining
to multiplication, division, and trigonometry correctly, despite
initially getting Q9 (a division question) wrong. Thus, we can
hypothesize that Tom is more likely to provide a correct response
to Q17. Andy, on the other hand, made errors in questions
related to multiplication, division, and trigonometry, indicating
potential challenges in mastering all the three concepts. This
observation suggests that students who lack proﬁciency in mul-
tiplication and division typically struggle with trigonometry as

In light of the preceding considerations, in this article, we
proposed a method called feature crosses information-based KT
(FCIKT), which introduces higher order crosses information
between the questions and the latent concepts to the previous
GKT. To be speciﬁc, we employ graph convolutional networks
(GCNs) [18], [19] to derive embedding representations of ques-
tions and latent concepts from multirelational heterogeneous
graphs (MRHGs) that encompass students, questions, and latent
concepts. Subsequently, we combine the difﬁculty information
of questions with the previously extracted embeddings to per-
form feature crosses operation, yielding richer higher order
information. In addition, we employ multiheads attention [20]
with a forgetting mechanism to enhance the modeling of ques-
tions and latent concepts. We integrate the higher order crosses
information with the gated recurrent units (GRUs) as a clue to
trace the students’ knowledge state and assess their likelihood
of answering the new question correctly. We benchmark our
method against seven existing KT models on three publicly
available datasets. The experimental results demonstrate the
efﬁcacy and superiority of our proposed method in various
aspects.
In a nutshell, the main contributions of this article are sum-
marized as follows.
1) We introduce an innovative method to enhance the perfor-
mance of modeling students’ knowledge states by incor-
porating higher order feature crosses information into KT.
This approach also facilitates a more robust exploration of
correlations between questions and latent concepts.
2) We designed an effective mechanism that utilizes MRHG
and multihead attention based on forgetting to simu-
late more realistic scenarios of students responding to
questions. This mechanism offers a more efﬁcient GKT
approach.
3) Comprehensive experiments conducted on three real-
world datasets show that FCIKT outperforms the state-
of-the-art models. Additional investigations afﬁrm the
efﬁciency of our approach in enhancing the performance
of KT models.
II. R ELA TEDWORK
A. Knowledge Tracing
KT, a widely studied area in educational technology, fo-
cuses on modeling and understanding the process of knowledge
acquisition by learners. KT models typically consist of three key
modules: the knowledge representation module, the modeling
module, and the inference module.
Bayesian networks were employed for modeling and predict-
ing students’ cognitive states, termed as BKT. The model em-
ploys the hidden Markov model (HMM) [21] to associate con-
cepts with binary knowledge states (i.e., learned or unlearned).
It assumes that each question corresponds to a single concept,
rendering it incapable of addressing questions involving multiple
concepts or representing dependencies among different con-
cepts. DKT, which applies temporally “deep” ﬂexible long short
term memory networks (LSTM) [22] to KT tasks. This model
employs a multitude of artiﬁcial “neurons” to represent latent
knowledge states and their temporal dynamics, facilitating the
learning of a latent variable representation of student knowledge
from the data. In response to identiﬁed limitations in the DKT
model, Y eung and Y eung [23] highlighted two main issues: a
reconstruction problem and the volatility of prediction results
for the input sequence. Then, they propose an enhanced version
of the DKT model called DKT+, achieved by increasing the cor-
responding regularization term. Wang et al.[5] proposed a novel
framework for dynamic cognitive diagnosis, which integrates
educational priors from cognitive diagnosis (such as question
attributes and interaction functions) into deep learning-based KT
models. Liu et al. [24] proposed exercise-aware KT, exploring
both student’s exercise records and the content of exercises for
student performance prediction.
The DKT-based study exhibits superior performance when
compared with traditional probabilistic models, yet it also high-
lights signiﬁcant shortcomings, particularly in terms of inter-
pretability. This led to the birth of the most iconic dynamic
key–value memory networks (DKVMN) [25] for KT. DKVMN
introduces an external memory module [26] to store the knowl-
edge and update the student’s knowledge mastery accordingly.
It also provides speciﬁc information about students’ knowledge
states across various KCs. Sun et al. [7] proposed DKVMN
with rich features for KT. This approach incorporates students’
behavioral features and dynamic learning abilities into the deep
memory network framework, aiming to strike a balance between
personalized learning capacities and behavioral characteristics.
Building upon the transformer architecture [27], numerous
studies have endeavored to integrate an attention mechanism
into the KT model. The central idea revolves around learning
the attention weights of questions during a series of interactions,
enabling the prediction of the probability of correctly answering
the subsequent question, with due consideration to the questions’
relative importance. The pioneering work in this area is self-
attentive KT [28], which introduced an attention mechanism
into the KT models. It employs the scaled dot-product attention
mechanism originally proposed by V aswani et al.[27] to acquire
attention matrices through multiple attention heads.
All the aforementioned approaches substantially enhance the
performance of the KT task. These methods all treat the students’
exercise records as sequential data to be fed into the model. To
the best of the authors’ knowledge, various relational structures
frequently emerge in daily exercise records, including similari-
ties between KCs, similarities between questions, containment
relationships between questions and KCs, and so on. From a data

B. Graph-Based KT
Graph neural networks (GNNs) [29] excel in processing
graph-structured data by effectively capturing and leveraging the
relational dependencies between nodes to make predictions or
perform tasks. This capability has diverse applications, spanning
social network analysis, recommendation systems, and knowl-
edge graphs.
Nakagawa et al.[15] proposed GKT. This marked the pioneer-
ing integration of GNNs, known for their robust graph-structured
data representation capabilities, into the KT task. GKT con-
ceptualizes the underlying graph structure of KCs as graph
G =( V,E ), where the node V = {V1,V2,...,V n} denotes the
set of all KCs, and the edgeE ⊆V ×V denotes the relationship
of these KCs. Rethinking the KT task, they cast it as a time-
series node-level classiﬁcation task, leveraging conventional
graph learning techniques, such as message-passing GNNs for
resolution. Recently, to further explore the information em-
bedded in knowledge structures, Tong et al. [30] proposed
structure-based KT, it aims to capture multiple relationships
among knowledge structures, such as similarity and precedence
relationships, and to model the propagation of inﬂuence among
KCs. Xia et al. [31] proposed a novel multivariate graph KT
(MVGKT) framework based on GNNs. MVGKT incorporates
GCN and graph Fourier transform to capture both temporal
dependence and interstudent correlation for predicting student
performance.
To enhance the extraction of valuable higher order infor-
mation pertaining to questions and concepts, Y ang et al. [32]
proposed graph-based interaction model for KT (GIKT), which
constructs a question–concept bipartite graph and utilizes GCN
to integrate question–concept correlations through embedding
propagation. To address the challenge of sparse student data,
Lu et al. [9] proposed concept map driven KT. This approach
explicitly incorporates intrinsic information from instructional
concept maps, including educational relations and topological
information.
C. Feature Crosses
The concept of feature crosses initially emerged in recom-
mender systems [33]. It is employed to integrate users’ personal
information, behavioral data, and attributes of items to improve
recommendation accuracy and personalization. Feature crosses,
also known as feature combination, accomplishe a nonlinear
transformation of the sample space by multiplying two or more
features, enhancing the model’s nonlinear capabilities. Essen-
tially, feature crosses involve mapping samples from the original
space to a high-dimensional feature space through a nonlinear
mapping function, similar to the kernel trick [34] in machine
learning.
In the context of temporal dynamics, an earlier classical model
for learning feature combinations is factorization machines
(FM) [35]. FM extracts feature combinations through the inner
product of hidden vectors between feature pairs. It is capable of
estimating reliable parameters for prediction even when dealing
with highly sparse data. Factorisation-machine supported neural
networks (FNN) [36] was derived on top of FM, which ﬁrst
proposed an idea to enhance FM by initializing MLP [37] with
the hidden vectors learned from the FM model.
To capture both low-order features and high-order features,
Guo et al. [38] proposed DeepFM. The model comprises two
components: FM (responsible for fusing low-order features) and
deep neural networks (DNN) (responsible for fusing high-order
features), enhancing the accuracy of click-through-rate (CTR)
prediction. The Wide & Deep [39] model, the core implemen-
tation involves combining a linear model (Wide) and a deep
neural network (Deep) to capture both memorization and gen-
eralization, resulting in improved performance for large-scale
recommender systems.

IV . PROPOSED METHOD
Previous GKT models have primarily focused on capturing
higher order interactions between questions and latent concepts,
neglecting to incorporate question difﬁculty information within
the exercise sequences. In addition, these models have not ef-
fectively captured high-quality embeddings between question
features and latent concept features. To address this gap, this
section provides a detailed introduction to our proposed model,
termed FCIKT. The FCIKT model incorporates the feature
crosses higher order information between questions and latent
concepts into the KT task, augmenting the acquisition of stu-
dents’ knowledge states with question difﬁculty information.
An overview of the framework is presented in Fig. 2.T h e
main modules are MRHG embedding module, feature crosses
fusion module, knowledge state evolution module, and predic-
tion module. Importantly, the process of acquiring the student’s
knowledge state is trained in conjunction with the prediction
module, where question and concept embeddings dynamically
originate from the MRHG module. The details of FCIKT are
described in the following sections.
A. MRHG Embedding Module
Similar to previous approaches, FCIKT predicts student re-
sponses based on knowledge states and question representations.
Formally, the raw data features need to be transformed into de-
sired embedded information through the embedding layer. Our
FCIKT model utilizes embeddings to represent questions, latent
concepts, and student responses for each question in the exercise
records. These embeddings are denoted as: eq ∈R|Q|×d, ec ∈
R|C|×d, andey ∈R2×d, where|Q| and|C| represent the number
of questions and latent concepts, respectively, andd denotes the
embedding size. Departing from traditional single-relationship
graphs, we incorporate multiple relationships among students
and questions, questions and concepts, and concepts themselves,
forming an MRHG. Higher order interaction information be-
tween question and concept nodes is extracted by aggregating
node features using GCN’s multilayer message passing mecha-
nism.
We preprocess the original dataset accordingly to construct
an MRHG, as shown in Deﬁnition 3.2. On top of that, we
establish three metapath relationships as follows. Questions are
considered neighbors if they are either answered by the same
student (Q-S-Q) or cover the same latent concept ( Q-C-Q).
Likewise, latent concepts are neighbors if they are implied by
the question that is the source of that latent concept ( C-Q-C).
These three metapath relationships guide the utilization of the
constructed MRHG data as input, enabling the GCN model to
generate node embeddings within the MRHG. The embedding
layer in this module exclusively emphasizes the state infor-
mation of questions and latent concepts, treating students as
the bridge connecting questions. Thus, the question–question
relationship matrix and question–concept relationship matrix
play essential roles in the message passing process of GCN.

CHARACTERISTICS OF THE COMPARISON METHODS
BKT: BKT is an HMM speciﬁcally designed to model a
student’s latent knowledge state as a set of dichotomous vari-
ables in a sequence of exercises. The BKT model assumes that
once knowledge is acquired, it is retained, while accounting for
factors, such as guessing and slipping.
KTM: Knowledge tracing machines (KTM) [41] is a state-of-
the-art factor analysis model that leverages FM to interact with
various features for prediction, making it capable of handling
a wide range of feature types. To ensure experimental fairness,
only questions, latent concepts, and answers are used as side
information for comparison.
Multistate methods: These methods maintain one vector for
each concept to represent students’ knowledge states.
DKVMN: DKVMN has the capacity to utilize relationships
between latent concepts and directly output a learner’s pro-
ﬁciency level for each concept. DKVMN comprises a static
matrix referred to as the “key” for storing latent concepts and a
dynamic matrix called the “value” for storing and updating the
corresponding concept mastery levels.
Single-state methods: These methods maintain one vector to
represents a student’s knowledge state.
DKT: DKT employs an RNN model to analyze exercise
sequences and model students’ proﬁciency in latent concepts.
In addition, DKT holds the distinction of being the pioneering
model to apply deep learning to KT.
DKT+: Proposed by Y eung and Y eung[23], this is an extended
variant based on DKT. DKT+ redeﬁnes the loss function of
original DKT model by introducing three regularization terms,
improving the consistency of its prediction performance over
time-steps. The problem that DKT cannot reconstruct the ob-
served inputs is additionally addressed.
GIKT: GIKT represents the relationship between questions
and KCs as a graph to learn valuable embeddings for answer
prediction. GIKT assumes that a KC can be related to more
than one question, and a question can correspond to more than
one KC. GNNs aggregate embeddings from questions and KCs
based on their relationships in the graph. These embeddings for
each question in the sequence of interactions are then fed into
an RNN model for predicting the student’s response to the next
question.
GAKT: Graph attention knowledge tracing (GAKT) is a
variant of exercise-enhanced RNN with attention mechanism
(EERNNA) model [24]. Given that EERNNA relies on question
textual descriptions, which are often unavailable from public
datasets. Therefore, we utilize the input question embeddings
obtained through GCN aggregation as inputs to EERNNA and
compare them according to its framework design.
CoKT: CoKT [13] retrieves sequences of peer students with
analogous question-answering experiences, extracting interstu-
dent information. This information is then amalgamated with
intrastudent data to track students’ knowledge states and predict
their question-answering accuracy.

C. Implementation Details
Comparative experiments involving different model ap-
proaches were conducted within the tensorﬂow framework. The
embedding size for questions, latent concepts, and answers were
uniformly set at 100. The student’s knowledge state, represented
as a vector, was sized at 100. The initial learning rate is set
to 0.00025. In order to solve the problem that the loss of the
training set cannot be reduced to a certain level, we set the
learning rate decay rate to 0.92, so that the learning rate will
gradually decay as the training progress. During the embedding
propagation process, a maximum of three aggregation layers
were used, and a dropout rate of 0.8 was employed to prevent
overﬁtting. Extensive experiments demonstrated that the optimal
performance was achieved with a batch size of 6. All models
are trained on a Linux server equipped with a 2.90 GHz Intel
Xeon Gold 6226R CPU and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 3090
GPU.
FCIKT also considers the higher order feature relationships
between questions and latent concepts. In the construction of the
attention network, our experiments revealed that considering the
similarity between questions is more effective than considering

E. Ablation Study
In this section, we perform ablation studies to investigate the
effectiveness of two vital components in our proposed model:
1) multihead attention mechanism and 2) feature crosses that
integrate question difﬁculty. We set up three comparison settings
and the details are shown below.
1) Remove multihead attention (RMHA) does not consider
dynamic semantic information in students’ question solv-
ing behavior.
2) Remove feature crosses (RFC) indicates that there is no
feature crosses operation for question, latent concept, and
difﬁculty embeddings.
3) Remove both multihead attention and feature crosses
(RBMF) directly concatenates eq,ec and ey as the ex-
ercise representation instead of using feature crosses and
multihead attention.
Except for the aforementioned modiﬁcations, the remain-
ing aspects of the models and experimental settings remain
identical.
We visualize the performance of these settings in Fig. 5.
We can observe that the performance of FCIKT consistently
deteriorates when any module of the model is removed. This
suggests the importance of all these modules to performance.
Notably, the removal of feature crosses from FCIKT has a more
pronounced impact on performance compared to the removal of
multihead attention. This observation leads to the hypothesis that
feature crosses, which incorporates information about question
difﬁculty, contribute more to feature acquisition, while multi-
head attention primarily inﬂuences the student’s knowledge state
in response to the question. Hence, the absence of the multihead
attention module has a milder effect on performance. It is also
worth noting that removing both multihead attention and feature
crosses during training signiﬁcantly impairs the performance of
FCIKT, providing further evidence that both of our proposed key
modules are crucial for enhancing the model’s performance.

associations distribution of question among the latent concepts.
Real-world educational data encompasses intricate relationships
among questions and diverse concepts. FCIKT is strategically
poised to grasp these high-order interactions for achieving pre-
cision in modeling students’ cognitive states and performance
prediction.
VI. C ONCLUSION
In this article, we introduce a novel model to address the
KT problem. The model utilizes a feature crosses approach to
integrate questions, latent concepts, and question difﬁculty in
a sequence of student exercises. This enhances the richness of
the embedding representations extracted using GCN, surpass-
ing previous GKT methods. At the same time, the multihead
attention mechanism, which integrates the forgetting behav-
ior, is also used to add dynamic semantic information to the
static embedding to simulate more realistic question answering
scenarios. Therefore, it can better predict students’ learning
performance based on both rich embedding representation and
dynamic knowledge state. We compare the performance of
our proposed model with the state-of-the-art KT model on
three public datasets. The results demonstrate the superiority
and interpretive effectiveness in modeling student performance,
validating its potential to be applied in real-world educational
settings. In the future work, we plan to improve and analyze the
model by using optimization ideas on node feature engineering.
